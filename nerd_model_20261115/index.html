PARTICLE NERD MODEL · BOX · SPHERE · CYLINDER

Closed-loop comparison with implicit MPM.

The same objects, motions and sand bed are simulated twice: once with implicit MPM and once with the Particle NeRD Local model coupled to the same rigid-body solver. Each case has a simulation video, a plot video, a surface-distance heat-map video and a kinetic-energy figure.

1. Protocol

Model. particle_nerd_local, checkpoint fb443cbd06b4e9d3… (training step 12,000), trained on 800 object and free-fall trajectories. All cases not marked fine-tuned use this checkpoint, with no retraining or tuning.

Fine-tuned model. The six sand-block cases marked fine-tuned use a second checkpoint (d594e78bb52797c6…): the model above, fine-tuned for 4,000 steps on a reduced mix of the original data (box, sphere and cylinder with free bodies, box with prescribed motion, and the vertical free-fall drops) plus 160 additional free-fall blocks that are dropped or tossed with a random horizontal speed. Its update rule lets the inertial, collider and gravity terms switch fully on or off. A control fine-tuned with the original update rule on the same data gave the same toss results. The closed-loop object cases on this page still use the first checkpoint.

Scenes. Tasks IsaacContrib-ParticleNeRDLocal-{Box,Sphere,Cylinder}-v0 (Particle NeRD) and …-MPM-v0 (implicit MPM), one environment each, 200 Hz physics, seed 42. Sand: 20,646 particles on a 0.032 m lattice in a 2.0 × 1.2 × 0.30 m tray, density 1,600 kg/m³, friction 0.48. Every closed-loop run starts with a 3 s hover over the bed, then performs the motion; both simulators use the same schedule. The object is a dynamic rigid body in all cases; the sand's reaction acts on it.

Motions. Straight push, circular push, vertical stamping at 2 Hz (to mimic legged contact) and a free drop with a 1 m/s sideways throw. Separately, sand-only blocks (cube, sphere, cylinder of particles, 0.40 m) are dropped or tossed onto open flat ground with no rigid body.

Videos. Simulation: the Isaac Lab viewer recording of the two runs side by side (the sand-only tosses use the Isaac Lab task IsaacContrib-ParticleNeRDLocal-Particle-<Shape>-<Motion>-v0 and its -MPM-v0 twin). Plot: a side slab of particles coloured by displacement, and the top-view change in surface height, drawn from the saved particle states. Surface distance: the signed distance from the Particle NeRD surface to the MPM surface, defined below.

Surface distance. Particle masses are deposited on a shared 0.02 m grid and smoothed with a 0.032 m Gaussian kernel; the surface is the 800 kg/m³ iso-surface (the same reconstruction as the benchmark's surface metric). For each upward-facing point of the NeRD surface the heat map shows the distance to the MPM iso-surface, positive where the NeRD surface lies outside the MPM material (higher or protruding) and negative where it lies inside (lower). The curves below the map are the mean and 95th-percentile absolute distance over all plotted points. The colour scale saturates at ±40 mm.

Kinetic energy. Particle speeds are finite differences of the saved positions over 20 ms, and the kinetic energy is ½ m v² with m = 0.0524 kg per particle. The figure has two panels: total kinetic energy over time (log scale), and the share of time-integrated kinetic energy at each z position (the world-frame vertical coordinate; the tray floor is at −0.30 m and the original bed surface is near 0). They show whether the energy of the sand is distributed like MPM's, not only whether the surface shape agrees.

Limits: one run per case and one seed. Although both simulators run the same 3 s hover, a roughly uniform height offset of about 5–6 mm between the two surfaces is already present before the object touches the sand (visible at the start of the distance curves). Its cause has not been established, and it is not removed. The sand material is a simulation setting, not calibrated to a measured specimen.

4. What the runs show

Driven motions (push, circle, stamp). The sand yields under Particle NeRD in all three shapes, with a trench and a berm in front of the object. Compared with MPM, the NeRD surface is disturbed more: by about 19–28 % in RMS height change for the straight push, 16–25 % for the circle, and 1.7–2.3 times as much for stamping.

Free drop. With nothing holding the object, it comes to rest near the surface in MPM and sinks through the bed in Particle NeRD (final height 16–19 cm below the MPM object). The crater keeps deepening for the whole run. The model supports a falling object much less than MPM does.

Tossed sand blocks. Dropped blocks spread almost identically in the two simulators. Tossed blocks do not: in free flight MPM keeps the 1.0 m/s sideways speed, while the NeRD cloud's sideways speed falls to nearly zero within 0.2 s, before anything touches it. The training free-fall data contained vertical drops only. After fine-tuning on tossed blocks (the cases marked fine-tuned), the sideways speed is kept (about 1.0 m/s at 0.1 s, as in MPM), the centre of mass ends at +0.25 m against +0.26 m for MPM, the kinetic energy integrated over time is within about 2 % of MPM's, and the final particle position error falls from about 370 mm to about 40–60 mm. A control fine-tuned with the original update rule on the same data gives the same result, so the improvement comes from the training data and not from the update rule. The sinking of resting beds and dropped objects is not fixed by this change.

Kinetic energy. Dropped sand blocks carry the same time-integrated kinetic energy in both simulators (within 3 % for the cube, sphere and cylinder). Tossed blocks carry about 64–65 % of MPM's, which follows from the lost sideways momentum. For the objects driven into the bed, Particle NeRD carries 70–84 % of MPM's energy in the straight push and 55–63 % in the circular push, but 4–18 times as much while stamping and 5–8 times as much during the free drop. In the box-stamping figure NeRD's energy sits nearer the surface while MPM's reaches deeper into the bed; that comparison was not checked for every case.

These are observations from single runs. The causes are not yet established, and no model changes have been made.

5. Working interpretation (not yet tested)

This section records the current reading of the results. It is a hypothesis to be tested, not a conclusion.

Reading. This version of Particle NeRD still does not predict vertical (z) particle motion accurately enough, and it does not produce enough contact force to support the object. The model predicts the particles under the object continuing to fall toward the bottom of the bed. The analytical contact estimator builds its force from those predicted particle velocities, so it then behaves as if the material were very soft. The model therefore fails at the particle–collider interaction itself.

What the free-drop runs measure. Particles that start within 8 cm of the surface under the object's final footprint, mean vertical displacement at the end of the run, and the layer next to the tray floor (initial z < −0.25 m):

In MPM the particles under the object settle within the first 0.8 s and then stay. In Particle NeRD they are still moving down at the end (box: −49 mm at 0.8 s, −69 mm at 1.6 s, −77 mm at the end), and the whole column compresses, including the layer next to the floor. This matches the first two steps of the reading. It was not measured here whether the contact estimator returns a low force; the force estimate was not logged in these runs.

Two candidate causes.

  • Feature aggregation. The local particle and boundary aggregation may not register the collider's presence strongly enough for particles under a heavy object, so the network does not see the contact that should stop them.
  • Global token prediction. The Transolver's global slice tokens may fail to carry the load from the object down through the bed, so the stop signal is not distributed to the particles below the contact.

Neither candidate has been isolated. A test that separates them would compare predicted vertical particle velocity under a held, static object against MPM while switching off one pathway at a time.
